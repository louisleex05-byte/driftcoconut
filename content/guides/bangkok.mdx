*Where century-old temples share sidewalks with midnight noodle carts, and the humidity comes free.*

## Quick facts

- **Best time to visit:** November–February (peak), March–May (shoulder), June–October (low, wet)
- **Cost per day:** budget $20–30 / mid-range $50–80 / luxury $200+
- **Airports:** BKK (Suvarnabhumi, ~25–30 km from center), DMK (Don Mueang, ~14 km from center)
- **Currency:** Thai Baht, roughly 33 THB to the US dollar this August
- **Time zone:** UTC+7, no daylight saving, ever
- **Language:** Thai, English fine in tourist zones
- **Visa:** 60 days visa-free for US/UK/EU/AU/JP has held through this summer, but a cut to 30 days is signed off and just waiting on paperwork — check before you book
- **Suggested trip length:** 5–7 days first time, 3 if you're squeezed

## When to go

I tell people December every single time, and I stand by it, but let's not pretend it's dry. Nights sit around 21–24°C, and you'll still sweat through your shirt walking to the 7-Eleven on Sukhumvit Soi 11 at nine at night — the humidity doesn't take December off just because the heat does. It's still the best window on the calendar. December and January are the driest months by far, a world away from September, which floods more days than it doesn't.

January runs almost identical to December, just a touch wetter — honestly treat them as one long good stretch rather than agonizing over which one to pick. March through May flips hot fast, highs pushing 35°C by April, and that's when I tell friends to plan around the pool, not around walking tours. June through October is the real rainy season, September worst of all, though the rain itself isn't the horror story people expect — it's a hard, loud downpour for an hour in the late afternoon, then the sun's back out and the street's dry within the hour.

Songkran runs April 13–15 in both 2026 and 2027, and I'd block the whole week, not just those three days — the water fighting starts early on Silom and Khao San and the city takes a day or two to properly dry out after. Loy Krathong moves with the moon: November 25, 2026, then November 14, 2027 — I got cocky once and assumed it was fixed, nearly missed the whole thing. And don't believe anyone who tells you hotel prices double during Songkran — the real number's closer to a 30% bump on average, with some budget spots actually discounting the week before just to fill rooms, while the five-stars sit at their normal high price and don't blink.

## Where to stay

Sukhumvit, the Asok and Phrom Phong stretch, is dense and easy and has had every ounce of neighborhood feel sanded off it by malls and condo towers. It suits anyone who wants zero friction and doesn't mind paying a little extra for the convenience. Mid-range runs $82–150 a night — the Aloft on Soi 11 sits right in that band and puts you an easy walk from Terminal 21. Nearest stations: BTS Asok, Nana, or Phrom Phong depending on your soi.

Riverside — Bang Rak and Charoenkrung — is the old-money side of town, colonial shophouses and gallery spaces backing straight onto the Chao Phraya, and it suits anyone who wants a slower, prettier base with a boat pier practically at the door. This is where the flagship five-stars cluster, so budget for that if it's the specific feel you're after. Nearest station: BTS Saphan Taksin, then a short walk to the pier.

Old Town, or Rattanakosin, puts you inside walking distance of the Grand Palace and Wat Pho, which matters if temples are the actual reason you came and not a box to check. Sala Rattanakosin looks straight across the river at Wat Arun and runs $62–172 a night depending on dates. Nearest access: MRT Sanam Chai, or the river piers directly. You give up restaurant range and nightlife staying here — that's the whole trade, and it's a fair one.

Here's the one I'll push back on: everyone recommends Sukhumvit to death, and I'd send a first-timer to Ari or Thonglor now instead. Ari's a few stops further up the same BTS line, leafy and low-rise in a way nothing near Asok has been for a decade, full of small coffee shops and neighborhood restaurants that Sukhumvit priced out years ago. Rooms run $55–140, usually cheaper than comparable Sukhumvit stock. Thonglor's the dressier version — Soi 55, rooftop bars, dense Japanese dining, its own BTS stop at Thong Lo. Either way you're giving up maybe 20–30 minutes of extra travel to the temple cluster. That's it. That's the whole cost.

## Things to do

Wat Pho opens around 8am, closes near 7pm, entry's 300 baht (about $9), no booking needed — get there early and the reclining Buddha hall is still quiet. Wat Arun's best right at sunset from across the river, or right at its own 8am opening if you want the terraces to yourself before anyone else climbs them; entry's 200 baht ($6), cash at the gate.

<GuidePhoto slot="activity" />

The Grand Palace is the one thing I'd book ahead for even though you technically don't have to — 500 baht ($14–15) covers the palace, Wat Phra Kaew, and the textile museum, open 8:30am to 3:30pm with last entry at 3:30 sharp. Go right at opening. This is also exactly where the "sorry, it's closed today" scam runs, and I'll never fully get over how well it works — the place has a 4.7 on Google, it's clearly not closing on a random Tuesday, and tourists still fall for it every single week. Walk to the actual gate yourself if anyone tells you otherwise.

Chatuchak needs a full morning, not an afternoon squeeze — Saturday and Sunday, 9am to 6pm, free to walk in, genuinely better before 11am before the heat and the crowd both spike at once. For the river, skip the packaged tour boats entirely and just hop the Chao Phraya Express for a few baht a ride, then pay for a private longtail through the Thonburi canals if you want the quiet side of the city nobody photographs.

A Chinatown food-walking tour beats a rooftop bar for the modern pick, honestly — rooftop bars are interchangeable from one hotel to the next, but a good Yaowarat guide takes you to stalls you'd never trust walking in cold, usually $30–60 a person.

Damnoen Saduak paired with the Maeklong railway market fills a day on its own — combined tours run $18–80, and the floating market's worth seeing before 9am, before it turns into a photo-op traffic jam.

## Getting around

From Suvarnabhumi, the Airport Rail Link runs 15–45 baht depending on distance, roughly 5:30am to midnight. From Don Mueang, the A1 bus to Mo Chit is a flat 30 baht, similar hours. If you'd rather skip the logistics after a long flight, book <AffiliateLink type="welcomePickups">Welcome Pickups</AffiliateLink> ahead — a driver with your name on a sign waiting outside customs is worth every baht the first night.

Around the city, BTS runs 16–59 baht a ride, or 150 baht for an unlimited day pass once you're hitting more than four or five stops. MRT sits in a similar 17–45 baht band. Grab covers everything the trains don't — cross-town rides usually land between 60 and 200 baht, cheap enough that I stopped bothering with tuk-tuks years ago. If you do get in one, agree the fare before you sit down; there's no meter to fall back on.

## Local tips + scams

Three things people get wrong constantly: don't point your feet at anyone or a Buddha image, don't touch anyone's head, and cover your shoulders and knees at temples — Wat Pho and the Grand Palace will make you rent a wrap cloth at the gate for 200 baht if you show up in shorts.

Beyond the palace scam, watch for the tuk-tuk "city tour" — a suspiciously cheap or even free ride that somehow always detours through a gem shop or a tailor, because the driver's earning commission whether you buy anything or not. And if a taxi won't run the meter, get straight back out — it happens constantly around Khao San Road and outside hotel entrances, which is basically the entire reason Grab exists.

<GuidePhoto slot="localTips" />

My real tip, the one only someone who actually lives here would hand you: boat noodles at Rot Fai Ratchada, after 8pm once the crowd thins and the stalls are still moving fast. Get four small bowls instead of one big one — that's how the dish is meant to be eaten. Cheap, cheaper than you'd expect for how good it is.

## FAQ

### Is Bangkok safe for solo female travelers?

Generally, yes. Daytime safety scores well across the city, and the real risk concentrates in nightlife strips like Khao San Road and Nana rather than everyday streets. Normal precautions apply here, nothing extra required.

### How many days do I need in Bangkok?

Three covers the essentials — Old Town on day one, a market and food on day two, one excursion on day three. Five lets you actually breathe between things. Seven lets you add Ari or Thonglor properly instead of rushing past them.

### Is Bangkok expensive?

Not really, by most standards. Backpacker days run $20–30, mid-range $50–80, and luxury starts around $200 if you're booking the named five-star towers.

### What's the best area for first-time visitors?

Sukhumvit wins on pure convenience — malls, BTS, everything close by. Ari wins on how the trip actually feels once you're there, at the cost of maybe 20–30 extra minutes to the temples.

### Do I need cash in Bangkok?

Yes, for street food, markets, and tuk-tuks — cards work fine at hotels and malls but not much beyond that. Withdraw a few thousand baht at once so you're not eating a fee on every small pull.

### What's the best month to visit Bangkok?

December and January are close to identical on weather, both sitting in that cool, dry window. I lean December, for the Loy Krathong afterglow and streets that are still a little quieter before the new year crowds land.
